Cellular Mechanisms of Hippocampal Plasticity

"Explain the cellular mechanism of Long-Term Potentiation (LTP) in the CA1 hippocampal region. Detail the pre- and postsynaptic events initiated during high-frequency tetanus, the critical channel gating dependencies, and the intracellular downstream signaling cascades culminating in synaptic strengthening."

Submitted Response• 482 words
Full CreditPartial Area

In the CA1 subfield of the hippocampus, high-frequency stimulation (tetanic delivery at ~100 Hz) induces sustained Long-Term Potentiation (LTP). Under baseline conditions, low-frequency glutamate release acts predominantly on postsynaptic AMPA receptors. However, upon persistent afferent stimulation from the Schaffer collaterals, repetitive glutamate release triggers massive AMPA receptor depolarization.

This local positive membrane potential is essential because it repels the electrostatically trapped bivalent cation within the pore of neighboring NMDA channels, achieving the critical NMDA Mg2+ block expulsion. With the voltage-dependent block cleared, the channel permits substantial Calcium (Ca2+) influx into the dendritic spine apparatus.

Once inside the postsynaptic microdomain, Calcium binds to calmodulin, subsequently activating calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII). CaMKII translocates toward the postsynaptic density where it phosphorylates substrates to enhance synaptic responsiveness and increase the total conductance state of AMPA channels.

Furthermore, downstream activation of neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS) stimulates the synthesis and retrograde transport of gaseous messengers. Through Nitric Oxide (NO) retrograde diffusion back across the synaptic cleft into the presynaptic terminal, soluble guanylyl cyclase is engaged, elevating presynaptic vesicle pool turnover and ensuring enhanced transmitter release probability for subsequent action potentials.
